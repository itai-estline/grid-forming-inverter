Build log, V1 to V13

I built the controller in thirteen steps. Each version adds one capability to the one before it and is saved as a new file, so a working model is never overwritten. This page lists what each version added and the result that told me it worked, or that it did not.

How the build went

Everything here is simulation and design work in MATLAB R2026a, Simulink and Simscape Electrical, with Ansys Mechanical and Autodesk Fusion for the packaging step. No hardware was built.

V1 to V10 were built in April 2026. In November 2026 I came back, ran a health check on all ten models, and found that some April results were wrong. The fixes, the packaging work (V11) and the last two controller versions (V12, V13) are from that second round.1 The stories behind the fixes are on What went wrong, and how I found it.

V1 to V4: a controlled voltage source

V1

Bare power stage

A 400 V DC source, a three-phase average-value converter, an LC filter of 2 mH and 20 ÂµF per phase, and an 8.4 Î© resistive load. There is no controller yet. A constant on the modulation input was only there to prove that the circuit solves.2 The converter is an average-value model, so the simulation never contains individual switching pulses.

Key result: the circuit runs for 0.1 s with the solver settings every later version keeps (ode23t, variable step).2

More: Power stage and filter

V2

Open-loop sine reference

A small MATLAB function makes three sine references, 120Â° apart, at 60 Hz, and feeds them to the converter. Nothing is measured and nothing is corrected. This is also where the line theta = omega * t first appears. At a fixed 60 Hz it is correct. Later it became a bug.2

Key result: clean three-phase 60 Hz sine waves at the filter output.2

More: Power stage and filter

V3

Closed-loop voltage

One PI controller per phase (P = 0.001, I = 0.1) compares the reference with the measured capacitor voltage. A one-sample delay in each feedback path breaks the algebraic loop.2 Much later I found that this loop was far weaker than I believed, and that its integrator put a DC offset on the output. V13 replaced it.3

Key result: stable three-phase output at 60 Hz over a 0.5 s run.2

More: The voltage loop

V4

Droop control

Key result: 59.9905 Hz with an 8.4 Î© load and 59.9820 Hz with 4.2 Î©. More load, lower frequency.4

V5 to V7: loads, faults and inertia

V5

R-L load: P and Q

The load became 8.4 Î© in series with 20 mH, so it draws reactive power as well as active power. That gave the voltage droop something to react to. The load impedance rises from 8.4 Î© to 11.3 Î©, so the active power drops and the frequency moves back toward 60 Hz. Both confused me at first and both are correct.4

Key result: P â‰ˆ 500 W, Q â‰ˆ 410 VAR, and the voltage command drooped from 346 to about 342 (model units), at about 59.995 Hz.4

V6

Fault ride-through

A 0.5 Î© fault is switched onto the output at 0.3 s, and a current limiter scales the modulation down when the measured current is over the limit. In April the limiter visibly cut in and I marked it done. In November I checked the number: scaling by limit / current is not a hard limit, and the fault current settled at 18.3 A against a 6 A limit.5

Key result, after the fix (model V6_cl, an integrating limiter with gain 2): fault current held at 6.02 A, inside Â±10 % of the limit 86 ms after the fault.5

V7

The droop block was replaced by the swing equation of a generator, JÂ·dÏ‰/dt = P0 âˆ’ P âˆ’ DÂ·(Ï‰ âˆ’ Ï‰0), with J = 0.5 and D = 150. V8 and V9: the side branch

These two versions start from V7 and go in a different direction. They taught me a lot, but the final model does not contain them, so they are side chapters on this site.

V8

dq-frame control

A Clarke and a Park transform turn the three sine voltages into two steady numbers, Vd and Vq, in a frame that rotates with the output. Two PI controllers then hold steady targets instead of three controllers chasing sines, and an inverse transform takes the result back to three phases. Getting it to work needed a normalising gain and retuned PI gains that the first plan did not have.7

Key result: Vd = 1.0 and Vq = 0.0 after a 2.0 s run, with symmetric sine waves and no DC offset.7

More: Side chapter: the dq frame (V8)

V9

PLL grid sync

Built on V8. An external 60 Hz grid source is added behind a breaker. A PLL measures the grid, a check compares the grid frequency with the inverter frequency, and the breaker closes at 0.5 s. I had planned an angle check as well, but the PLL angle and the inverter angle come from two independent generators, so their difference sweeps through a full turn and cannot be used. The check became frequency only, with a 0.6 Hz window.8

Key result: SYNC OK stayed at 1 for the whole 3.0 s run, and the breaker closed at 0.5 s with a single spike before the voltages settled back to clean sines.8

More: Side chapter: PLL grid sync (V9)

V10 to V13: two inverters, and a box to put one in

V10

Two inverters in parallel

Branches from V7, not from V9. Two copies of the inverter feed one shared bus, each through a tie of 0.05 Î© and 0.2 mH. They differ only in damping: D = 150 for inverter 1 and 300 for inverter 2, with P0 = 250 W each. There is no communication between them. The April run looked like a pass and was wrong in two ways, which is story 2 on the other page.9

Key result (corrected model, load step from 8.4 Î© to two 8.4 Î© loads in parallel): inverter 1 went from 404.0 W to 702.5 W, inverter 2 from 557.5 W to 1155.2 W, both at 59.837 Hz then 59.520 Hz. Î”Pâ‚‚ / Î”Pâ‚ = 2.003.9

Not a control model. V12

Current limiting in parallel

With a load too large for the pair, the two integrating current limiters (one per inverter) fought each other through the shared bus in a slow cycle. I tried three changes. Freezing the swing equation while limited did not stop it. An inductive virtual impedance made it worse. A virtual resistance did work: the controller subtracts RvÂ·i from its voltage command, and Rv grows with the over-current.11

Key result (overload): inverter 2 held at 10.14 Â± 0.15 A against a 10 A limit, no limit cycle, 2.56 kW delivered, and normal sharing unchanged at 2.003. Still open at this point: 181 VAR circulating between the units, and a bolted fault that settled at 11.4 A.11

More: Current limiting and fault ride-through

V13

Voltage loop and virtual impedance

The final model. The voltage loop was rebuilt in volts: a feed-forward of 1/200 plus a proportional gain of 0.002, and no integrator. The virtual resistance got an integral term so that a fault settles exactly on the limit. A virtual reactance, 0.75 Î© on inverter 1 and 0.375 Î© on inverter 2, was added so that the two units stop pushing reactive power at each other.12

Key result: Î”Pâ‚‚ / Î”Pâ‚ = 2.000 for the load step (385.2 W to 670.0 W and 520.3 W to 1090.0 W, 59.857 Hz to 59.554 Hz), 11 VAR circulating in overload, and 9.999 A and 10.001 A in a bolted fault. Voltage error at base load: âˆ’0.12 %.12
