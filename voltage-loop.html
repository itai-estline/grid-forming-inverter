The voltage loop

The virtual synchronous machine decides what the output voltage should be. The voltage loop is the part that makes the filter capacitors actually produce it. It is only two terms, a small proportional gain and a feed-forward, and the most useful thing I did to it was take an integrator out.

What the loop does

The controller upstream produces a reference for each phase: a sine of amplitude Vmag at the angle θ the swing equation gave it. The converter, filter and capacitor are the thing being controlled. The voltage loop looks at the voltage on each phase's filter capacitor, compares it with the reference, and adjusts the converter's modulation until the two match.

It works in the abc frame, on each of the three phases separately, so the signal it is chasing is an ordinary 60 Hz sine, not a constant. That matters for the integrator story further down. This loop is what makes my inverter behave like a voltage source: whatever the load does, the capacitor voltage is pushed back toward the reference.

I did not always have this. Until V13 the "loop" compared a 346-amplitude reference with a voltage scaled down by 150. Logging the controller output showed it never came near saturation and the voltage was not really being regulated: the loop was acting almost like a fixed gain1. V13 rebuilt it so the feedback is in volts and does real work.

The final controller

For each phase, the modulation command is the reference times a feed-forward gain plus a proportional correction on the error:

u = Kff·r + Kp·(r − y)

Here r is the reference in volts and y is the measured capacitor voltage in volts. The feed-forward gain is Kff = 1/Vdc,half = 1/200 = 0.005, because a modulation of 1 on a 400 V bus is worth 200 V. In other words the feed-forward alone says "to make r volts, ask for r/200", and it gets most of the way there. The proportional term, Kp = 0.002, only has to clean up what the feed-forward misses3.

There is no integrator. After this the command passes through a saturation (the converter cannot ask for more than the bus can give), the modulation stage and the converter. The reference amplitude at the output is V0 = 71.5 V, the value I logged at the capacitor in the simulation, and the Q–V droop moves it from there4.

Why Ki = 0

Every model on the V7, V10, V12 line had a PID block in this spot with an integral gain Ki = 0.1. An integrator is the textbook way to remove a steady error, so it looked harmless. But the thing it was integrating here is a sine. The integral of a sine is a cosine plus a constant, and the constant is set by where in the cycle the integration started. It never decays, and the feedback was too weak to push it out.

offset ≈ (A·Ki / ω)·cos φ0,   A·Ki/ω = 346 × 0.1 / 377 ≈ 0.092

When I logged the controller output for 0.3 s in V13 the offset was there: a DC component of +17.7 V on the phase a capacitor and −8.9 V on phases b and c, in both inverters5. That is about 2 A of DC into the 8.4 Ω load, and the phase A current measurement saw it too. The full story of how I found it, and why it was invisible for so long, is on the what went wrong page.

With Ki = 0 the offset is 0.000 V6. Total power at base load dropped from 964 W to 909 W when the DC component went away, which is a small reminder that the old results had been carrying a little extra current they should not have had6.

Choosing the gain

With the integrator gone, Kp is the only thing left to tune. I swept it at base load for 0.3 s, always with Ki = 0 and the feed-forward at 0.0056.

Tracking is already within 0.12 % at Kp = 0.002, because the feed-forward does most of the work. Going to 0.005 buys almost nothing, and 0.01 rings. So I took 0.002, which is a factor of five below the gain that rings. I also did not need the resonant term I had planned as a backup for a plain gain on a sine; the numbers never asked for it7.

One side effect worth knowing: V13 models run about 3.4 times slower than V12 (about 480 s per 0.3 s of simulated time with two runs in parallel), and that is independent of Kp6.

Where the virtual impedance is subtracted

The voltage loop also decides how the two virtual impedances reach the output. In V12 the virtual resistance was subtracted from the modulation, after the loop. Once the loop had real feedback it would have partly cancelled that drop, so in V13 I moved it into the reference: the loop's target is Vmag·sin(θ) − Rv·i − jXv·i, and the loop simply tracks that8. The virtual resistance limits fault current and is explained on the current limiting page. The virtual reactance keeps two inverters from fighting each other and is on the parallel operation page.

The sensor behind the feedback

In hardware the loop would need a measurement of each capacitor voltage. I did not design that part: the model simply reads the capacitor voltages. The 3D model shows where a voltage sense block would sit, near the filter capacitors.
