Side chapter: PLL grid sync (V9)

Before an inverter connects to a grid, it should know what the grid is doing. In V9 I used a phase-locked loop to measure an outside grid, checked that my inverter was close enough in frequency, and then closed a breaker at 0.5 s. Like the dq frame, it is a side branch and not part of the final V13 machine.

What a phase-locked loop does

A phase-locked loop (PLL) is a circuit, or a few lines of code, that watches an incoming sine wave and generates its own sine wave that stays in step with it. It does not just copy the frequency. It matches the phase, meaning where in its cycle the wave is right now.

It has three parts in a loop. A phase detector measures how far apart the two waves are. A PI controller turns that error into a frequency correction. An oscillator integrates the frequency into a phase angle. In equations, for a grid at angle θg and the PLL at angle θp:

e = sin(θg − θp),  ωp = ωnom + Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt,  θp = ∫ωp dt

While the error is not zero, the frequency is pushed up or down until it is. The integral term learns the frequency difference, so once the PLL has settled the phase error goes to zero even if the grid is not exactly at its nominal frequency. Try it below. The frequency is slowed down so you can watch.

What V9 built

V9 starts from V8 and adds an outside grid1. The grid is a three-phase voltage source at 60 V phase-to-phase RMS and 60 Hz, with its own source impedance. A three-phase breaker sits between my inverter's AC bus and that source. Voltage sensors go on the grid side of the breaker, because sensors on the inverter side only read the inverter's own voltage.

The PLL is Simulink's three-phase sinusoidal measurement block, with a PI loop filter (P = 0.1, I = 1), an initial frequency of 60 Hz and a sample time of 1e-5 s1. That sample time mattered: with the default inherited setting the discrete PLL never locked and reported zero amplitude for the whole run1. A step block closes the breaker at 0.5 s, and the run lasts 3.0 s, because the PLL takes two to three seconds to settle with those gains1.

The synchronisation check subtracts the inverter's frequency from the PLL's measured grid frequency and lights SYNC OK when the difference is under 0.6 Hz1.

The check I had to change

My first plan also required the phase angles to match, with a difference under 0.05. That cannot work here. The PLL angle and my inverter's angle come from two independent generators, so their difference sweeps through every value from 0 to 2π, and never stays near zero1. I dropped the angle test and kept only frequency. I also had to raise the frequency threshold from 0.1 Hz to 0.6 Hz, because the virtual synchronous machine runs at about 60.5 Hz when it carries load, so a 0.5 Hz difference is normal1.

So V9 is a weaker demonstration than the name suggests. SYNC OK only compares frequency, and the breaker closes at 0.5 s because I told it to, not because the check allowed it. That is why there is a spike when it closes. What the run does show is that the PLL measures the grid correctly and the inverter stays stable when it is connected.

What it showed

SYNC OK stayed at 1 for the whole simulation. The breaker closed at 0.5 s with a single spike in the transient, and the three-phase output voltage then recovered to clean, symmetric sine waves1. The PLL's amplitude reading settled near 49 and its frequency converged on 60 Hz1. The 49 is what a 60 V phase-to-phase RMS grid should read: 60 / √3 × √2 ≈ 49 V peak per phase.

How this relates to grid-following

Most inverters on the grid today are grid-following. They run a PLL all the time, take the angle it reports and inject current at that angle2. That works while there is a strong grid to follow. With a weak grid, or none, there is nothing to lock to, and the PLL itself can lose lock during disturbances2.

My inverter is the other kind. It makes its own angle by integrating the frequency from the swing equation, so it needs no outside signal to run. In V9 the PLL has a much smaller job. It only measures the grid, so I know whether it is safe to connect, and then the inverter carries on with its own angle. The PLL is a measuring tool here, not the thing the inverter depends on.

Why it is not in the final machine

V8 and V9 are a separate branch off V7. The final model, V13, follows V7 to the two-inverter model (V10) and then the current-limit and voltage-loop work (V12, V13), and none of that contains a PLL3. In V13 the two inverters line up with each other through the shared bus and their droop, with no outside grid to measure. The dq-frame side chapter is the other half of this branch, and the build log shows where both sit among the thirteen versions.
