Grid-forming in the real world

My design is one small inverter in a simulation. This page is about why that kind of inverter matters on real grids, which real projects and grid codes exist, and what is still unsolved. I checked every outside fact against a source and linked it. Where I could not, I describe things in general terms.

Where your power comes from

Follow the power backwards from a phone charger. It comes through the wall from the distribution lines on your street, which are fed by a substation. The substation is fed by high-voltage transmission lines, and those start at a step-up transformer beside a power plant. In North America that whole system runs at a nominal 60 Hz1.

Solar panels and batteries make DC. Wind turbines make AC at a frequency that changes with the wind. Neither can be connected to the grid directly. An inverter (or a converter that does the same job) sits between the plant and the transformer and turns what the plant makes into grid-quality 60 Hz AC. That makes the inverter the gateway: every unit of solar, battery and wind power passes through one.

The old heartbeat, and why it is fading

For a century the grid's frequency has been held by big spinning machines. Every steam, gas and hydro generator is a heavy rotor locked to the grid at 60 Hz. If demand suddenly jumps, the rotors slow down a little and give up some of their stored motion to cover it. That stored motion is inertia, and it acts like a shock absorber: it makes frequency change slowly.

When a large generator trips or demand jumps, frequency starts to fall. How fast depends on the total inertia on the system. Grid operators describe this as the rate of change of frequency (RoCoF), and lower inertia means a higher RoCoF after the same event. If it falls too fast there is not enough time for the frequency controls to act2.

Solar panels and batteries have no rotor. As they replace spinning plants, the system has less inertia unless something supplies it another way. AEMO, the Australian market operator, names the retirement of synchronous generators alongside more inverter-based resources as a reason for the new problems in system strength, frequency and voltage control3. My own version of the inertia idea is on the inertia and droop page, where a control loop in software plays the part of the rotor.

Follow or form

Nearly all inverter-based resources on today's grids use grid-following control, according to a NERC white paper4. A grid-following inverter measures the grid's voltage, locks onto its phase with a phase-locked loop (a PLL), and pushes current into it. In circuit terms it behaves like a current source. It needs a voltage to follow. If the grid is weak or gone, it has nothing to lock to.

A grid-forming inverter works the other way round. It produces its own voltage and frequency and lets the current be whatever the load takes, so it behaves like a voltage source5. NERC puts it as controls whose main aim is to hold an internal voltage phasor nearly constant over the first fractions of a second after a disturbance4. Both NERC and AEMO say there is no single agreed definition yet46.

My design is a former. Its swing equation and droop law let it set its own frequency and share load with a second unit. That is all explained in the pages that follow this one.

What forming unlocks

  • Islanded microgrids. A group of loads cut off from the main grid still needs something to hold the voltage and frequency. A forming inverter with a battery can do that on its own. A follower cannot.
  • Black start. Black start means restoring a power station or grid after a shutdown without drawing on the external network7. NERC lists blackstart as a possible capability of some grid-forming inverters, not all of them4.
  • Weak grids. A weak grid (low system strength) is one where the voltage moves a lot when current changes. Followers can become unstable there because their PLL tracks a wobbling voltage. NERC recommends operation in low system strength conditions as a grid-forming capability4, and AEMO says forming inverters have the potential to address some of the system-strength problems3.
  • Sharing without talking. With a droop law, each unit lets its frequency sag a little as it takes more load, so several units split a change between them with no communication. That is what my two-unit result shows at small scale.

Real installations

Three projects I could check against a source. I did not build or test any of them. These are only examples of the technology in use.

Hornsdale Power Reserve, South Australia

Hornsdale is a large Tesla battery. Its expansion added 50 MW / 64.5 MWh, and ARENA, Australia's renewable energy agency, helped fund it with A$8 million of a A$71 million project8. The expansion was used to trial a software mode in which the battery behaves like a synchronous machine and gives a virtual inertial response. The project reports call it virtual machine mode. Trade press describes the same thing as a grid-forming inverter providing inertia9.

Blackhillock, Scotland

Zenobe's Blackhillock battery was announced as live on 3 March 2025. The first phase is 200 MW / 400 MWh, with grid-forming inverters from SMA, and it provides stability services (inertia and short-circuit level) to NESO, the GB system operator10. It is one of the grid-forming batteries bought under NESO's Stability Pathfinder. Zenobe's later project page says a second phase is still being commissioned11, so I give only the first phase.

Kauai, Hawaii

On Kauai the utility, KIUC, runs a small island grid with a lot of solar and storage. After an oil-fired generator tripped in late 2021, frequency dropped and oscillations appeared in the first minute. Three of the four solar-plus-storage plants involved used grid-following inverters. KIUC then moved to grid-forming controls at its solar-plus-storage plants, and the US national lab NREL (now the National Laboratory of the Rockies) tested the island grid and reported that grid-forming resources significantly improved stability12. I could not open the lab's own page, so this one rests on a news report of it. I give no plant sizes because I could not confirm them.

Grid codes and standards

Rules are starting to name grid-forming capability. Three developments I could verify:

  • AEMO (Australia), May 2023. A voluntary specification, version 1.0, that sets out the core capabilities a device should have to be called grid-forming, plus extra desirable ones. It is voluntary, and AEMO says it is meant to inform future rule changes and procurement6.
  • Great Britain, February 2022. Grid Code change GC0137 added a minimum specification for grid-forming capability. It is not mandatory13.
  • Europe, 2025. ENTSO-E published a technical report in November 2025 on grid-forming requirements under the draft NC RfG 2.0, the update to the EU connection rules for generators. The requirements in the draft are described as non-exhaustive, and the report itself is non-binding14. I could only confirm that the code is a draft, so I say nothing about when it takes effect.

The NERC white paper of September 2023 is a recommendation, not a rule: it offers a working definition and a list of functions to ask of grid-forming batteries4. My own work makes no grid-code compliance claim. I never tested it against any of these documents.

What is still unsolved

NERC says understanding of how grid-forming units affect the bulk power system is still at an early stage, and that each unit has to be studied and tuned for its system15. Three open problems line up with things I ran into in my own simulations.

Fault current

A voltage source will try to push as much current as a fault asks for, and power electronics cannot carry that. NERC's wording about fault ride-through is conditional: it asks for adequate fault current "if hardware limits allow"15. In my model the limit is 10 A, measured as a one-cycle average of the phase current. In the final version (V13) a bolted fault held the current at 10.0 A16. The trick was a virtual resistance that switches on only when the current is over the limit. Getting there took several versions, which are described on the current limiting page. A simulated inverter holding a limit does not mean a real one would, because the first cycles of a real fault are faster than my software loop.

Many units, one grid

Two units are easy to describe. In V13 they shared a load step exactly 2:1, matching their droop gains of 300 and 15017. A real grid has thousands of units from different makers with different controls, and their interaction is a research question. The parallel page shows how even two units needed extra care.

Standards and testing

There is still no agreed definition, and the rules above are mostly voluntary or draft. Meeting one document does not settle everything either. Transgrid, an Australian network owner, states that meeting AEMO's voluntary specification does not by itself guarantee a battery can provide its own voltage-waveform support service18.

To be clear about scope: this project is simulation and packaging design. No hardware was built, and I make no claim of grid-code compliance or certification.
