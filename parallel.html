Two inverters in parallel, and virtual impedance

A grid is many sources on one set of wires, and none of them can ask the others what to do. I put two copies of my inverter on one bus in simulation. They split a load change exactly 2 to 1 with no communication. Making them also stay out of each other's way in reactive power took a piece I had not planned: a virtual reactance.

The setup

Two identical inverters, each with its own 400 V DC source, converter and 2 mH / 20 µF filter, connect to one shared bus through a tie of 0.05 Ω in series with 0.2 mH per phase. The load hangs on that bus: 8.4 Ω per phase at the start of the sharing test1. The inverters differ in two settings: the damping D (150 for inverter 1, 300 for inverter 2) and the virtual reactance, which I come to below.

Sharing with no communication

In steady state my virtual synchronous machine reduces to a droop line, P = P0 − D·Δω, which the virtual inertia and droop page derives. Both inverters sit on the same bus, so they see the same frequency, so Δω is the same for both. The one with D = 300 therefore changes its power by exactly twice as much as the one with D = 150. Neither unit knows the other exists. The shared frequency is the only message.

The test: the base load is 8.4 Ω, and at 0.5 s a second 8.4 Ω is switched in next to it, which doubles the load on the bus. I ran it for 2.0 s and read the powers just before the step and at the end3.

The change in inverter 2 divided by the change in inverter 1 is 569.7 / 284.8 = 2.000. The hand check uses the droop line with the measured frequency, and lands within 1 W of the simulation. The reactive powers at the end of the step were 34.3 and 21.2 VAR, under the 50 VAR target3.

Slide it past 100 % and the split stays at 1 to 2, because that is all the droop line says. The real inverters stop obeying it once the current limit takes over, which is the overload case below.

Circulating reactive power

Two voltage sources that are slightly different will push reactive power back and forth between them without it ever reaching the load. I call that circulating reactive power: half the difference of the two units' Q, averaged over 0.1 s. My target for it, under the +4.2 Ω overload, was below 50 VAR. It took four steps to get there.

  1. V12, 181 VAR. The baseline with virtual resistance for current limiting. I recomputed this number from the saved run when an older value would not reproduce5.
  2. V13 with a working voltage loop, 308 VAR. Worse. Making the voltage loop stiff regulated each capacitor voltage. That took the 2 mH filter inductor, which is 0.75 Ω at 60 Hz, out of the path between the two voltage sources, leaving only the tie of 0.05 Ω and about 0.075 Ω of reactance. A few volts of mismatch then drove hundreds of VAR. V12's weak voltage loop had been hiding the filter inductor in that path. The Q–V droop was not to blame: with it switched off the number was 872 VAR6.
  3. Equal virtual reactance of 0.75 Ω, 51 VAR. Put back a defined impedance between the units by adding a virtual one equal to the filter reactance7.
  4. Scaled virtual reactance, 11 VAR. Inverter 1 gets 0.75 Ω and inverter 2 gets 0.375 Ω8.

A larger equal reactance does not help: 2 Ω gave 90 VAR and cost about 15 % of the delivered power (2.08 kW)7. The scaling is the useful part. Inverter 2 carries twice the current, so with equal reactance its virtual voltage drop is twice as big, and the difference pushes reactive power onto inverter 1. Giving inverter 2 half the reactance makes the two drops equal, and the reactive power splits evenly.

How the virtual reactance is built

A reactance jXv·i leads the current by 90°, and in a three-phase system there is a neat shortcut for that without any derivative. For a positive-sequence set of currents, (ic − ib)/√3 is the phase A current advanced by 90°, which is j·ia. So each phase's inductive drop is a fixed combination of the other two phase currents, scaled by Xv/√3. A small matrix does it for all three phases at once, the result goes through a one-sample delay, and it is subtracted from the voltage reference9.

vref,a = Vmag·sin θ − Rv·ia − Xv·(ic − ib)/√3

Unlike the virtual resistance on the current limiting page, which is zero until the current passes 10 A, this reactance is always on. The same expression with the phase indices rotated gives phases b and c.

The overload test

To push the pair past their rating I added a +4.2 Ω step. With the final virtual reactance, the two inverters stayed phase-locked: the phase difference did not drift over the last second and the averaged frequency difference was 0.000 Hz. Inverter 2 settled at 9.74 A ± 0.06 A and inverter 1 at 5.69 A8.

That 9.74 A is below the 10 A limit, so in the final design this test no longer reaches the current limiter. The virtual reactance itself lowers the current enough. It means the overload run does not prove the limiter works. The bolted-fault test on the current limiting page does8.

A misdiagnosis worth keeping

An earlier overload run, with two integrating current limiters, had the two frequencies reading 59.80 and 59.74 Hz after 2 s, and I wrote it down as the two inverters falling out of step. They were not. Integrating the frequency difference showed the phase offset stayed within a few degrees, and the real problem was a limit cycle, with each limiter cutting its voltage while the other picked up the current, about every 0.12 s10. The full story is on the what went wrong page.

The unit these two copies are made of

Everything above is simulation of a controller and a circuit. The design stops at one packaged unit, below, and the second inverter in the tests is simply another copy of it. No hardware was built.
