Virtual inertia and droop

A grid-forming inverter has to decide its own frequency. Mine does it with a virtual synchronous machine: a few lines of math that behave like a spinning rotor. This page explains why a grid wants inertia, how the swing equation supplies some, and why two of these inverters share load without talking to each other.

Why a grid needs inertia

The traditional grid is run by synchronous generators: big machines whose rotors all turn in step with the grid frequency, 60 Hz in North America. Supply and demand on a grid have to match at every instant. When they don't, for example when a large load switches on, the missing power has to come from somewhere right away, before any control system has reacted.

It comes from the rotors. They are heavy and they are spinning, so they store kinetic energy, and they give some of it up by slowing down slightly. Rotor speed and grid frequency are locked together, so the frequency drops with them1.

How fast it drops depends on how much spinning mass there is. With a lot of inertia the frequency slides down slowly, and the plants have time to add power. With little inertia the same disturbance makes the frequency fall quickly. Engineers call that speed the rate of change of frequency, and a grid that moves too fast is hard to keep stable.

Solar panels and batteries have no rotor. An inverter that only follows the grid adds no inertia. So as spinning plants are replaced, the same bump shakes the frequency harder1. A grid-forming inverter can fill that gap by behaving, on purpose, like a machine with mass.

The virtual synchronous machine

The part of my controller that does this is called a virtual synchronous machine (VSM). It has three steps: measure the power, run the swing equation, and turn the resulting frequency into a phase angle.

1. Measure P and Q

A three-phase power sensor at the inverter output gives the active power P and the reactive power Q. Each one goes through a moving average that is one cycle long (1/60 s), which removes the ripple and leaves a steady number2.

2. The swing equation

This is the equation that describes how a real generator's rotor speeds up and slows down. I use it with made-up mass:

J·dω/dt = P0 − P − D·(ω − ω0)

Here ω is the inverter's frequency in rad/s and ω0 = 2π × 60 is the nominal value. P0 is a power setpoint and P is the measured power. Read it like this: if the load takes more power than the setpoint, the right side goes negative and the frequency starts to fall. J decides how quickly it is allowed to fall, like the mass of a rotor. The D term pushes back harder the further the frequency is from 60 Hz, so the fall stops somewhere.

Getting these numbers took a few tries. My first attempt in V7 used D = 10 and P0 = 0. With P0 = 0 the equation treats the whole load as an excess and drives the frequency down until it hits the clamp at 54 Hz, and D = 10 was not enough damping3. P0 has to be close to the power the inverter really delivers: 495.75 W for the single inverter in V7, and 250 W each once two inverters share the load4.

Two things to try. Raise J and the frequency takes longer to move: that is inertia. Raise D and it does not fall as far: that is damping. In this simple model the time constant is J/D. With my values that is 0.5 / 150 ≈ 3.3 ms, shorter than the one-cycle power measurement, so at the default settings the measurement sets the pace.

With the default settings the start and end frequencies match V13, because they depend only on the steady state, which the next section explains. The path in between is the simplified part. In Simulink, with both inverters and the network, the frequency settled about half a second after the step5.

3. Phase is the integral of frequency

The voltage reference is a sine of an angle θ, so the frequency has to become an angle. The angle is the running integral of the frequency, wrapped to stay between 0 and 2π:

θ = ∫ 2π·f dt

My April models computed θ = ω·t instead. That is the same thing only if the frequency never changes, and the whole point of this controller is that it does6. The bug helped a wrong result look right until November. The full story is on the what went wrong page.

Droop: sharing load by leaning

Once things settle, the frequency stops changing, so dω/dt = 0 and J drops out of the swing equation. What is left is a straight line:

P = P0 − D·(ω − ω0)

This is droop, and D is the droop gain. For every rad/s the frequency sits below nominal, the inverter delivers D more watts. Two inverters on the same bus see the same frequency, so the one with the larger D takes more of the load. Inverter 2 has D = 300, twice inverter 1's 150, so it picks up twice the change. Neither unit needs to know the other exists. The shared frequency is the only message.

Here is the check with numbers. In the V13 load step the load on the bus doubles (a second 8.4 Ω is switched in next to the first), and both inverters go from 59.857 Hz to 59.554 Hz7. That is about 0.3 Hz for twice the load. At 59.554 Hz:

Δω = 2π × (59.554 − 60) = −2.802 rad/s

P1 = 250 + 150 × 2.802 = 670.3 W    P2 = 250 + 300 × 2.802 = 1090.7 W

Plain droop came first

Before the VSM there was V4, which used droop with no inertia at all: the frequency was set directly from the measured power, f = f0 − Kp·P, with Kp = 0.00001 Hz/W. A single inverter ran at 59.9905 Hz with an 8.4 Ω load and 59.9820 Hz with 4.2 Ω8.

These are different models with different gains, so the two columns are not a like-for-like comparison. What they share is the shape: more load, lower frequency, along a straight line. The VSM adds the rotor in front of that line.

The same idea for voltage

Droop runs a second time for reactive power, with voltage in place of frequency:

Vmag = V0 − Kq·Q

V0 is 71.5 V and Kq is 0.00715 V/VAR. I chose Kq so that 500 VAR lowers the voltage by 5 %: 0.05 × 71.5 / 500 = 0.007159. An inverter that supplies more than its share of reactive power lowers its voltage a little, which pushes reactive power toward the other unit. At base load there is almost none to share (5.3 and −1.1 VAR)10, and after the load step the two units carry 34.3 and 21.2 VAR7.

This droop existed in every version since V4, but before V13 it did almost nothing, because the voltage loop underneath it was not really regulating the voltage. Fixing that is the subject of the voltage loop page. Once it worked, I could measure its effect: in an intermediate V13 build, switching the voltage droop off under overload raised the reactive power circulating between the units from 308 VAR to 872 VAR11. Getting from 308 VAR down to the final 11 VAR took something else, a virtual reactance, which is on the parallel operation page.

Where this would run

In hardware, all of this would run on a small controller board with a microcontroller. I did not design that board or choose a processor. The algorithms are fully specified in the V13 model. The hardware they would run on is not.
